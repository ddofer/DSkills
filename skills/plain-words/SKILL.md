# Plain words

Write for a smart colleague who wasn't in the project. Use words they already know, or define a word once, in plain words, where it first appears. This skill covers words and sentences; for what to say and in what order, see `writing-for-readers`.

## Rules

- **One idea per sentence.** Judge by meaning, not punctuation. Split where the reader would have to hold two claims at once; keep a tight pair together ("X rises while Y stays flat"). Prefer the active voice: "we compared", "users finish".
- **No telescoping.** Don't make the reader hold a growing chain of qualifiers before the point arrives: noun stacks, "which… where… that…" chains, brackets inside brackets. Name the thing, then say what happened to it. Give each qualifier its own sentence, or drop it if the reader doesn't need it.
  - Before: "The redesigned flow's Q3 mobile returning-user median checkout time fell 12%."
  - After: "We looked at returning customers on phones, July to September. With the new checkout, half of them finished in a time 12% shorter than before."
- **Say it the ordinary way.** Use what someone outside the project would call it: "the third of players who favour shock most", not "the high-appetite tercile" or "the boldest third". No metaphors standing in for a measurement.
- **Use the field's standard term** ("odds ratio", "95% interval"), never one you invented.
- **Shorter when just as clear:** "edgier answers win more", not "a positive association between transgression intensity and selection probability"; "use", not "utilize".
- **One name per thing, everywhere:** text, tables, figure labels and captions. Three names read as three things.

## Cut

- **Coined labels** ("regulars", "end fifths", "the dial", "arms", "main set"). Replace each with what it measures.
- **Text only the writer needs:** file names, variables, job ids, internal section labels, notes for the next agent, provenance bookkeeping. Move sources to a sources list; delete the rest.
- **Hedge words:** "it seems possible", "to our knowledge", repeated disclaimers.
- **Labels seeded by notes.** Project notes, memory and glossaries spread coined labels into later writing. When you replace one, fix it at the source too, and list the old name as one to avoid.

## Keep

- **What each number is.** Plain is not vaguer, and rewording never changes a number. Check each plain phrase against the source. A corrected estimate is not the raw measurement: "the halves correlate 0.57, which implies 0.73 for all the data", not "the halves agree at 0.73". A loose everyday word can mislead: "a random pick" suggests pure chance, so say "picks drawn from the model's probabilities". Put the technical name in a footnote.
- The user's own terms.
- A defined term that saves real space, defined once where it first appears.

Done when you have read it as an outsider and changed every word you would have to ask about.
